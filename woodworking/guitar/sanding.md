# Sanding

## Wet vs dry

Wet sanding and dry sanding are two methods of surface finishing that use the
same principle—abrasive friction—but with different effects and purposes. Here’s
how they compare:

⸻

1. Method • Dry sanding: Uses sandpaper without any lubricant. It relies on
   friction alone to remove material. • Wet sanding: Uses water (or sometimes a
   mix of water and a bit of detergent) as a lubricant to reduce friction and
   carry away debris.

⸻

2. Surface Finish • Dry sanding: Produces a rougher surface. It’s good for fast
   material removal—like stripping paint, shaping wood, or leveling filler. •
   Wet sanding: Produces a much smoother, finer finish. It’s often used in the
   final stages of work (e.g., automotive paint polishing, guitar finishing, or
   high-gloss woodworking).

⸻

3. Dust and Cleanliness • Dry sanding: Creates a lot of airborne dust, which can
   clog sandpaper and pose health or cleanliness issues. • Wet sanding: Keeps
   dust trapped in the water, reducing airborne particles and extending
   sandpaper life.

⸻

4. Sandpaper Type • Dry sanding: Uses traditional sandpapers (aluminum oxide,
   garnet, etc.). Not designed to get wet. • Wet sanding: Uses waterproof
   “wet/dry” sandpaper—usually silicon carbide—bonded with resin that doesn’t
   degrade in water.

⸻

5. Heat and Friction • Dry sanding: Generates more heat; can burn surfaces
   (especially paint or wood) or melt plastic edges. • Wet sanding: Cooler and
   gentler, minimizing the risk of overheating and warping.

⸻

6. Speed and Effort • Dry sanding: Faster material removal; efficient for rough
   shaping. • Wet sanding: Slower, more controlled; ideal for refinement and
   polishing.

⸻

7. Common Uses • Dry sanding: Wood shaping, drywall, paint removal, metal prep
   before primer. • Wet sanding: Automotive paint finishing, clear coats, metal
   polishing, fine woodworking finishes.

⸻

8. Summary Table

Low Heat Higher Lower Finish Rougher Smoother Sandpaper Regular Wet/dry
(waterproof) Typical Use Early stages Final finishing/polishing

⸻

In short: dry sanding is your fast, rough sculptor; wet sanding is your patient
polisher. One gets the job done quickly, the other makes it look flawless.

## Sanding during finishing

Dry sanding (bare wood): Start around 120 grit → move to 180 → 220 → 320 before
sealing. Higher than 320 on raw wood tends to burnish the surface, which can
stop finishes from adhering evenly.

Wet sanding (finish work): Once the clear coat has cured (and cured means hard,
not just dry to the touch), begin around 800 grit wet/dry, then progress through
1000 → 1500 → 2000. Finish with polishing compound and a soft cloth or buffer.

## Avoiding burnishing wood during sanding

When woodworkers talk about “burnishing the surface,” they mean the wood fibers
have been compressed and polished smooth by friction rather than cleanly cut by
the abrasive.

Here’s what’s actually happening: When you sand wood with very fine grits (often
above 320–400), instead of removing more material, the abrasive particles start
to press the fibers flat. The surface looks silky and reflective, but that
smoothness comes from compression, not refinement.

⸻

Why it matters • Finish adhesion suffers. Burnished wood doesn’t absorb sealer
or stain properly. The pores are effectively sealed by heat and pressure, so
finishes sit on top instead of soaking in evenly. • Uneven color. You’ll often
see “blotchy” stain or patchy lacquer because some areas are too slick for the
finish to grab. • Hardwoods show it most. Dense woods like maple or rosewood
burnish easily; soft woods like spruce or mahogany less so, but it can still
happen.

⸻

How to avoid it • Stop sanding bare wood around 220–320 grit. That’s the sweet
spot where the surface is smooth but still has a bit of tooth. • If you think
you’ve burnished it (the surface feels glassy or squeaky under your hand),
lightly scuff it with a fresh 220 grit or a fine Scotch-Brite pad to reopen the
pores before finishing.

⸻

Burnishing isn’t “bad” if you’re intentionally going for a hand-rubbed, oil-only
finish—it can give a soft luster. But if you plan to apply lacquer,
polyurethane, or stain, it’s like trying to paint over waxed glass: everything
looks fine until it doesn’t stick.

It’s one of those small details that separates a finish that looks good from one
that glows.
