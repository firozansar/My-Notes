## Anatomy of a piece of wood

- Good mental model for wood is a large collection of straws bound together.
- Anatomy is defined relative to the grain of the wood
    - end
        - soaks up glue like straws wicking up water - joints not as strong
        - grain direction is perpendicular to the surface
        - the surface is cut across the grain i.e. 90deg/perpendicular to the
          grain
        - like looking at a the end of a collection of straws bound tightly
          together
    - face
        - usually what is visible in the constructed object
        - grain runs along the length of the board
    - edge
        - most wood is not square so has narrow sides and wide sides
        - edge is the narrow side of a board
        - usually parallel to the grain
            - the grain lines should run (ideally) parallel to the long edges of
              the edge rectangle
        - usually you cut tongue and grooves into edges
        - glue joints are strong because the bond long-grain to long-grain
- Anatomy impacts
    1. How you should cut the wood
    1. How you glue it and join it to other pieces
    1. How the wood will expand/contract over time
- The ideal board has all grain lines running from end to end (like a collection
  of straws bound into a rectangle) This is quarter sawn and is expensive. Most
  wood is not like this.

## How wood expands and contracts

Moisture/humidity is what causes wood to expand and contract because the wood
both absorbs **and** releases moisture into the surrounding air.

The cells in the wood absorb water when the humidity rises. They release it when
humidity decreases.

- Movement along the grain = negligible
    - 1m board might only change 1mm through a full humidity cycle
    - wood cells don't get taller when they absorb water - they get fatter
- Movement in radial direction = moderate
    - from the centre of the tree outward
- Movement tangential (around the grain) = largest, can be 2x radial movement

A number of joint types account for movement e.g. tongue and groove with gaps to
allow seasonal expansion and contraction

> [!WARN] If you joint wood at different grain directions then you joint needs
> to account for one part expanding while the other does not. Similarly jointing
> wood to things which don't move like metal.

In theory if the moisture content of the wood never changed then it would not
move. In practice we can try to minimise it but not eliminate it. Varnish, oil,
paint can **slow** the exchange of water but it cannot stop it.

Wood is anisotropic (= not isotropic = not same in all directions) - it is
stable along the grain but alive across it.

Water moves up and down the straws much more easily than across them in dry wood
(because the pits collapse to closed during drying)

Water travels well into end-grain but can also enter the cells through the pits
on the side of the tubes. How well this happens depends on how much the pits
have closed up during drying.

> [!TIP]
> This implies that the more dry wood is (the more the pits have collapsed) the more resistant to water along the grain it is

### Wood cells

- long tube like structures
- purposes
    - carry water and nutrients up the tree
    - provide physical support
- shape and size varies by species
- softwoods
    - 95% tracheid cells
        - 3-5 mm long, 0.03 - 0.05 mm wide
        - long slender cells with tapered ends
    - looks like bundles of drinking straws tied together
    - have holes in the sides called pits - fluids like resin and water can move
      in/out through the pits i.e. move sideways from one cell to another
    - have different geometries depending on which part of growth cycle they
      were created in
        - spring wood = larger internal cavity (lumen) and thinner walls
        - summer/late wood = smaller lumen, thicker walls
    - softwoods do have parenchyma but a lot less than hardwood
- hardwoods
    - more complex, more cell types:
        1. fibres
            - strength
            - 0.5 - 1.5 mm long (so visible with naked eye)
        1. vessels/pores
            - water transport
            - wide tubes 0.05 - 0.5m across, up to 1mm long
            - these form the visible pores on the end grain
            - some hardwood pore cells are the same size all year (e.g. maple),
              some vary depending on growth season e.g. oak
                - it is the pores that form visible growth rings
        1. parenchyma
            - brick shaped storage cells
- The tubes are glued together with lignin
- Rays run perpendicular to most other cells
- Pitts end up sealed/closed in dry timber
    - the pit membranes collapse or the torus seals against the pit border,
      effectively turning the wood’s plumbing system into a sealed bundle of
      straws.
    - you can "somewhat" re-open them a bit - this can make the wood accept
      finish or glue better
    - closed pits can be a problem
        - stain doesn't take evenly
        - resin infusing doesn't work evenly
        - dense or resinous woods resist glue penetration
        - end grain doesn't soak finish evenly
    - methods
        - heat/steam
        - vacuum pressure
            - used in industrial applications when we want to impregnate the
              wood with something e.g. resin/glue
        - steam/boil the wood
        - hot water or alcohol soaking
        - oxidative or solvent cleaning - removes surface clogging from resinous
          species
            - light alcohol wipe just before applying a penetrating finish
            - solvent clean oily wood before glueing to stop surface resin from
              stopping the glue from penetrating
        - light sanding/scraping after re-moisturising with damp cloth
            - water can reopen the pits and raise the grain making it more
              receptive to stain/finish

> Understanding the pits and connectivity helps explain phenomena like “wood
> won’t soak finish deep unless you seal or pre-treat” — many of the pits are
> closed/sealed in dry timber.
